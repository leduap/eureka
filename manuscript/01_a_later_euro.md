# 1. A later euro is worth less

Martin puts a one-page note on the table. The trade fair is in four weeks. A supplier will hold the price of a calibration line until the Friday before the fair, and not after.

The line costs €2,400,000, paid before it is switched on. It needs €300,000 of parts on the shelf, and those parts come back as cash at the end, if the line runs its five years. Each year, for five years, Leonie is to assume:

- extra revenue of €1,500,000
- extra cash costs of €720,000
- depreciation of €480,000, which is the €2,400,000 spread evenly, with nothing left at the end

"We have always used 8 percent," Martin says. "My father likes a round hurdle. Tell me if we should buy it. I need it before lunch."

This is the first question of the course. A euro in five years is not a euro now. You may not add the five years together and compare the sum with the price.

**Write this down.** At 8 percent, do you buy the line? One word, yes or no. If you want to show off, write the net present value to the nearest thousand euros.

## What the course is doing

Depreciation is not a cash cost. It is the spreading of a cash cost you already counted when you wrote the cheque. Count it twice and you will reject good machines.

The cash the line produces in a year is the profit after tax, with the depreciation added back.

Operating profit is €1,500,000 − €720,000 − €480,000 = €300,000.

Tax at 30 percent is €90,000. Profit after tax is €210,000.

Cash is €210,000 + €480,000 = €690,000. Same answer, every year, in this case, because the case has been kind enough to hold the world still.

You give up €2,700,000 today: the machine and the parts. You receive €690,000 a year for five years, and you receive the €300,000 of parts back at the end.

At 8 percent, five equal payments of €1 are worth €3.9927 today. Five payments of €690,000 are worth about €2,755,000. The parts, discounted five years, are worth about €204,000. Against €2,700,000 given up, the line is worth about €259,000 more than it costs.

The ledger, which does not round in the middle, says **€259,145**.

Yes. You buy it. The course would give you the marks.

**Your number.** Do it again at 6 percent, still in your head if you can. The five-year factor at 6 percent is 4.2124. Is the line more attractive, or less?

It is more attractive. A lower hurdle makes future euros heavier, not lighter. The ledger's net present value at 6 percent is **€430,708**. Martin has been using a hurdle that is harder than the one the next chapter will build. He has been rejecting lines that clear a fair rate. That is a family habit, not a theorem. Write it in the margin. You will need it in February.

## Where the case goes quiet

Leonie turns the page over. The back is blank. The blank is the point.

The €2,400,000 leaves in January and February. The €300,000 of parts leaves in February. The first customers do not pay in February. Hartfeld's customers pay in about forty-six days, and these are new customers of a new line, so she is told to assume sixty.

Cash today is €2,400,000. The machines take all of it. The parts take €300,000 that the firm does not have.

**Write this down.** On the morning after the parts are paid for, and before a single customer has paid, what is the bank balance?

It is **€300,000 overdrawn**. The line has a positive net present value and a negative bank account. Both statements are true. The course, as usually timed, asks only the first.

Sabine Keller at the house bank will care about the second. She does not get a vote in a net-present-value exercise. She has a vote in whether the wages go out in March.

## Three hands

Jonas says: borrow the €300,000. The value is €259,000. A few months of interest cannot eat that. Do the deal, fund the hole, move on.

Amina says: put the financing inside the project. If the overdraft costs 5 percent and the hole lasts four months, the interest is small. Show it as a line in the case so the board sees that you noticed.

Piotr says: the parts are not the hole he would worry about. The hole he would worry about is a customer who loves the new line and pays in ninety days, or a supplier of the machine who wants the second half of the money when the thing is still in a crate. "Your sixty days is a hope."

Piotr is the one to listen to, and Jonas is not wrong about the size. Interest on €300,000 for four months at 5 percent is €5,000. It does not overturn €259,000. The case fails, if it fails, because the receipt does not come, not because the discount rate was 8 instead of 6.

The Monday rule is one sentence. **Do not approve a positive net present value until you have named the month the bank balance is lowest, and the person who covers it.**

## Check

1. Cash from the line each year is €690,000, not €300,000 and not €210,000.
2. At 8 percent the net present value is €259,145. You buy the line.
3. At 6 percent it is €430,708. A lower hurdle helps the project.
4. The bank balance, before any customer pays, is €300,000 overdrawn. Cash of €2,400,000 minus machines of €2,400,000 minus parts of €300,000.

If your net present value was a few thousand either side, your factor was rounded. The decision does not change. If your net present value was negative, you subtracted depreciation twice, or you discounted the depreciation as well as the cheque.

## In the room

The question is the capital-budgeting question. Will you commit cash today for cash later, and did you count only cash? The page these cases often skip is the month the cash is gone and the customers have not started paying.
