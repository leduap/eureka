# 2. Risk has a price

The calibration line is still on the table. So is a second sheet, which Martin found in a drawer of his own enthusiasm. A young engineer wants Hartfeld to sell a smart gauge: a measuring tool with a subscription, a battery, and a marketing plan.

"Same company," Martin says. "Same 8 percent. If the first one cleared, price this one the same way."

The gauge costs €1,800,000. It ties up €500,000 in parts and returned goods, back at the end of five years if the fashion ends neatly, which is the case being polite again. Depreciation is €360,000 a year. The cash coming back, after tax and with depreciation added back, is €500,000 a year. Leonie checks that against the operating profit so she trusts it: operating profit €200,000, tax €60,000, profit after tax €140,000, plus €360,000 of depreciation, is €500,000. Good.

**Write this down.** At 6 percent, the rate from the end of the last chapter, is the gauge a yes? At 12 percent, is it still a yes?

## One rate is a claim about the firm

The course builds a rate in three pieces.

The safe euro, for this book, is 3 percent. It is a teaching rate, a stand-in for what a government bond pays, not a forecast.

The extra for owning the market, rather than the safe euro, is 5 percent. Also a teaching rate. People argue about this number for whole careers. You need a number to multiply, so the course picks one and moves.

Hartfeld's shares, if they were traded, would be assumed to move a bit less than the market. The course calls that a beta of 0.90. Cost of the family's equity is 3 percent + 0.90 × 5 percent = **7.5 percent**.

The bank's money costs 5 percent before tax. After the 30 percent tax shield on interest, it costs 3.5 percent. This is the sentence that causes fights in week two. The shield is real only if there is profit to shield. Hartfeld has profit. You may use it. You may not use it on a firm that is about to be loss-making. Chapter 3 is that story. For now the firm is healthy.

Debt is €8,000,000. Equity on the books is €13,200,000. The course uses those book figures because there is no share price. Say so out loud whenever you do it. You are using a stand-in.

Value of the firm, on that stand-in, is €21,200,000. Equity is 62 percent of it. Debt is 38 percent.

Weighted average cost of capital:

62 percent × 7.5 percent, plus 38 percent × 3.5 percent = **5.99 percent**.

From here on, this book calls it **6 percent**, so the later chapters divide cleanly. The unrounded rate is 5.99 percent. On a decision this close, show both.

The asset beta, if you care, unlevered with the tax shield, is 0.63. You do not need it to answer Martin. You need it the day someone tells you the firm's beta and the gadget's beta are the same thing.

## The gauge is not the firm

At 6 percent the gauge's net present value is **€179,811**. The course-trained answer is yes.

At 12 percent it is **−€213,898**. The answer is no.

Nothing in the cash flows changed. The claim about risk changed.

A smart gauge with a subscription is closer to a consumer product than to a caliper that workshops replace every fifteen years. Leonie writes a beta of 1.60 next to it. She labels it a judgment. There is no regression. Pretending there is one would be a better-looking lie.

Cost of equity at that beta is 3 percent + 1.60 × 5 percent = 11 percent. Blend with the same after-tax debt and the gauge wants something near **8.2 percent**, not 6 and not Martin's old 8. At 8.2 percent you would still run the numbers rather than wave. At 12 percent, which is a blunt way of saying "this can fail in a heap," the gauge destroys value.

**Your number.** Which error is more expensive: using 6 percent on the gauge, or using 8 percent on the calibration line?

Using 6 percent on the gauge accepts a project the harsher rate rejects, a swing of nearly €400,000 between the two net present values, and the downside is a product Hartfeld has never sold. Using 8 percent on the calibration line rejects, or nearly rejects, a project that is worth €259,000 even at the harsher family hurdle and €431,000 at 6 percent. The false yes on the new product is the worse error. The false hurdle on the old product is how family firms get slow. Both are the same mistake. One rate, pasted onto every idea in the building.

## Three hands

Jonas says: take the gauge at the company rate. "You cannot have two hurdle rates. The board will think you are moving the goalposts."

Amina says: two rates, and a page that shows the board what beta you assumed. If they hate 1.60 they can say so. Silence is not an assumption.

Piotr says: he does not believe the €500,000 a year. A subscription business has returns, churn, and a server. None of those are in the note. "Price the risk after you know what the cash is. You have priced a story."

Amina has the classroom right. Piotr has the job right. Jonas has the politics right and the finance wrong. A board that is allowed only one rate will use it on the exciting project, because the exciting project is the one somebody loves.

The Monday rule. **The discount rate belongs to the project, not to the letterhead.**

## Where the case goes quiet

Book equity is not the value of the family's claim. Chapter 6 will put a value on Hartfeld well above €13,200,000. If the equity is actually worth more, the weight on the cheap debt falls, and the 6 percent rises. The rate in this chapter is good enough to choose between two machines. It is not good enough to tell Ingrid what her 20 percent is worth. Do not let it travel.

Also, 3 percent and 5 percent are choices. Change the market extra from 5 percent to 6 and every yes in the building gets a little harder. Sensitivity is the grown-up version of a hurdle rate. One number, with a note of what would change your mind.

## Check

1. Cost of equity for Hartfeld is 7.5 percent. The weighted average is 5.99 percent, used as 6 percent from here on.
2. The gauge is a yes at 6 percent (€179,811) and a no at 12 percent (−€213,898).
3. The calibration line was a yes even at 8 percent. The family hurdle was strict. It was not the same mistake as pricing the gauge like a caliper.

Build the firm's rate once, in this order, and do not skip a line.

1. Safe euro, 3 percent.
2. Extra for the market, 5 percent.
3. Equity costs 3 + 0.90 × 5 = 7.5 percent.
4. Debt costs 5 percent, and 3.5 percent after the 30 percent shield.
5. Weights, from the books because there is no share price: equity €13,200,000, debt €8,000,000, together €21,200,000.
6. 62 percent × 7.5, plus 38 percent × 3.5 = 5.99 percent, called 6 from here on.

**Write this down.** If you decide the firm's beta is 1.20, not 0.90, what is the cost of equity, and does the weighted average stay under Martin's old hurdle of 8 percent?

Cost of equity becomes 3 + 1.20 × 5 = **9 percent**. The weighted average becomes **6.92 percent**. Martin's 8 percent is still the harsher number. The argument is not that 8 was cruel. The argument is that one number was being used for a caliper and a gadget.

## What to say when someone demands a regression

There is no share price, so there is no regression. Say that in the first sentence, or a clever person will invent one for you out of Nordmess and call it Hartfeld. A peer's beta is a clue. It is not a measurement of this family's cash. Label the 0.90 and the 1.60 as judgments, put your name next to them, and let Klaus change them in the meeting. A judgment Klaus has seen is a better rate than a regression nobody checked.

## The rate, built slowly enough to break

Students lose this chapter in the third line, not the last. They can recite "weighted average" and still not know which number is a judgment and which number is a weight. Build it as six sentences you could defend if Klaus interrupted after any one of them.

The safe euro is 3 percent. It is not a forecast of next year's bond, and it is not the rate Sabine charges. It is the stand-in for a euro you are treating as safe, so that every other euro in the building can be described as that euro plus something for trouble. If you change it, you change every project in the same direction. That is why it is a poor place to hide a dislike of the gauge. A dislike of the gauge belongs in the gauge's beta, where Klaus can see it.

The extra for owning the market is 5 percent. Careers are spent on whether it should be 4 or 6. You need a number that multiplies, so the course picks one and refuses to apologise for the pick on every page. What you may not do is pick 5 for the calibration line and 8 for the gauge and call both of them "the market." The market extra is the same for every project. The project's own trouble is the beta. Mixing those two up is how a room ends up with a rate it cannot explain a week later.

Hartfeld's beta of 0.90 is a judgment that the family's claim, if it traded, would move a little less than the market. Workshops replace calipers slowly. The firm is not a fashion business. Say the reason in the same breath as the number, or the number will be edited by whoever speaks next and the reason will be lost. Cost of equity is 3 + 0.90 × 5 = 7.5 percent. There is no tax in that line. Students put the tax shield on the equity cost because they have just heard that tax matters. Tax matters on the debt, because interest is the deductible thing. The family's return is not deductible to the family in this construction. Leave 7.5 alone.

The bank's 5 percent becomes 3.5 percent only after you have said, out loud, that there is profit to shield. Hartfeld's operating profit is €6,000,000 and the interest is €400,000. There is profit. The shield is allowed. The sentence to store for chapter 3 is that a firm about to lose money does not get this courtesy. A rate that assumes the shield in a loss-making year is a rate that has counted a tax saving the tax office will not pay.

The weights are the part everyone wants to skip, because they look like accounting. Debt €8,000,000. Book equity €13,200,000. Together €21,200,000. Equity is about 62 percent of that stand-in. Debt is about 38 percent. The ledger's unrounded shares are 0.6226 and 0.3774. Use 62 and 38 in the room if you say they are rounded. Use the unrounded shares if the decision is close. Then:

about 62 percent of 7.5 percent, plus about 38 percent of 3.5 percent, is **5.99 percent**.

Call it 6 from here on, and keep 5.99 in the footnote of your own head. The rounding is a kindness to later divisions. It is not a claim that you measured the rate to the hundredth and then threw the hundredth away because it was inconvenient. On the gauge, the hundredth is not the thing that flips the decision. The beta is.

The asset beta, unlevered, is the trouble in the business once you have taken the debt's trouble back out. The ledger's figure is 0.63, unrounded 0.6319. You do not need it to answer Martin this morning. You need it the day someone says "the firm is 0.90, so the gauge is 0.90." Those are not the same object. One is a claim about the family's shares, debt and all. The other is a claim about a product that has not been built. Unlevering is how you stop yourself handing the product the family's capital structure by accident.

## What 0.90 is, and what it is not

There is no share price, so there is no chart of weekly moves, and there is no regression. A regression on a price that does not exist is a costume. Nordmess has a price. Someone in the room will offer you Nordmess's beta by lunchtime and call it Hartfeld's, because the products rhyme. A peer's beta is a clue about the kind of business. It is not a measurement of this family's cash, this family's debt, or this family's habit of refusing loans. If you take it, label it as taken, and then stand next to it with your own judgment. Klaus is allowed to say that 0.90 is too brave or too timid. He is not allowed to hear that a computer measured it.

Try the judgment he is most likely to make. He thinks the firm is riskier than Leonie does, and he says the beta is 1.20. Cost of equity becomes 3 + 1.20 × 5 = **9 percent**. The weighted average becomes **6.92 percent**. His old hurdle of 8 percent is still harsher than the rate the method builds, even after you have let him win the argument about beta. That is the useful surprise in the exercise. The fight is not "8 percent was cruel." The fight is "one number was being asked to describe a caliper and a gadget, and it was the family's mood, not a blend of anything."

Write both rates on the page he will see: 5.99 at a beta of 0.90, and 6.92 at a beta of 1.20. Then write his 8 beside them. The page teaches him that his habit is a choice on top of the method, not the method itself. Family habits can be good. They should have to introduce themselves.

## The gauge, rebuilt from the cash up

Martin's second sheet can be made to show its working, which is the only way to trust the €500,000 of cash. The gauge is assumed to sell €2,000,000 a year. Cash costs are €1,440,000. Depreciation is €360,000, the €1,800,000 spread over five years. Operating profit is €200,000. Tax at 30 percent is €60,000. Profit after tax is €140,000. Add back the depreciation and the cash is €500,000. Same trick as the calibration line. Different business.

You give up €2,300,000 today: the €1,800,000 of machine and design, and the €500,000 tied up in parts and returns. You receive €500,000 a year for five years, and you hope to receive the €500,000 of parts back at the end, if the fashion ends neatly. The case is being polite again. A subscription product does not end neatly. Say that before you fall in love with the arithmetic.

At 6 percent the net present value is **€179,811**. At 12 percent it is **−€213,898**. The swing between those two, nearly €400,000, is the entire content of the chapter. The cash did not change. The claim about how badly the cash can fail changed. A yes at the firm's rate and a no at a blunt "this can fail in a heap" rate is not a close call you should wave through because the first number was positive. It is a call that depends on whether you believe the gauge is a caliper.

Leonie's judgment is a beta of 1.60. Cost of equity 11 percent. Blended with the same after-tax debt, the ledger's rate for the gauge is 8.17 percent, which the chapter has been calling near 8.2. At that rate you still run the numbers rather than inherit the firm's 6 or Martin's 8. The 12 percent is not a measurement. It is a way of saying: show me the project that survives even if we are harsh. This one does not.

The unlevered beta of that judgment is 1.12 in the ledger's rounding, 1.1234 unrounded. You still do not need it for the meeting. You need it if someone tries to lever the gauge with a different amount of debt than the firm carries, and then compares the result with 0.90 as if both numbers were the same species. They are not. Keep the species straight and the meeting gets shorter.

## The circularity, said once

A careful person notices a loop. The weights use the value of equity. The value of equity, in chapter 6, will depend on the rate. The rate depends on the weights. Book equity breaks the loop by being the wrong object on purpose. It is a stand-in you can compute on a Monday without pretending you already know what Ingrid's shares are worth. The stand-in is good enough to choose between two machines. It is not good enough to tell Ingrid what her 20 percent is worth, because chapter 6 will put the family's claim well above €13,200,000, and a heavier equity weight would push the cheap debt's influence down and the 6 percent up.

Say this limit in the meeting where the rate is born, not in the meeting where someone tries to spend it on a sale of shares. A number that travels further than its label is how a firm ends up discounting a person's life savings at a rate that was built to choose a milling machine.

Sensitivity is the grown-up version of the same humility. The 3 percent and the 5 percent are choices. Move the market extra from 5 to 6 and every yes in the building gets a little harder, including the caliper. Move only the gauge's beta and only the gauge gets harder. Those are different edits. A room that cannot say which edit it just made will "stress test" a project by changing three things at once and then announce that the project is sensitive. Of course it is. You shook the whole building. Change one thing, write down whether the yes survived, and then change the next thing.

## What she puts in the file

Two rates, on one page, with the judgments in ink and the arithmetic in a place Martin can re-add.

The firm's cost of equity at a beta of 0.90 is 7.5 percent. Debt after tax is 3.5 percent, and only because the firm has profit. Weights are book weights, because there is no share price: equity €13,200,000, debt €8,000,000. The blend is 5.99 percent, called 6. If Klaus insists the beta is 1.20, equity costs 9 percent and the blend is 6.92 percent. His 8 percent hurdle is still harsher than either blend. It is a family rule on top of the method. It is allowed. It should not be used as if it were the method.

The calibration line is a yes at 6 percent (€430,708) and a yes at 8 percent (€259,145). The gauge is a yes at 6 percent (€179,811) and a no at 12 percent (−€213,898). A beta of 1.60 on the gauge is a judgment, not a regression. There is no share price to regress. Nordmess is a clue, not a measurement of this firm.

The false yes is the expensive error: taking the gauge at the firm's rate. The false no, rejecting calipers because the family likes 8, makes the firm slow. Both errors are the same sentence used twice. One rate, pasted onto every idea in the building.

She does not, in this note, tell Ingrid what 20 percent is worth. The note says the rate is not for that. Chapter 6 is for that. A file that keeps its numbers in their chapters is a file that can be reopened without a fight about which meeting they belonged to.

## How a marker reads the page

Full marks require the six lines in order, the two net present values of the gauge, and a sentence that book equity is a stand-in. Half marks are a correct 5.99 with the shield added to the equity cost as well, which double-counts the tax and flatters every project. Zero, for the purpose of this session, is a single rate defended on the grounds that the board would be confused by two. The board's confusion is a real constraint. It is not a theory of value. You may present one rate in the pack if you have computed two and can say what the second one would have done. You may not skip the second one because the pack has a house style.

The question Jonas asked, whether two hurdles look like moving the goalposts, is a question about trust. Answer it with the page, not with a single number chosen to look consistent. Consistency with a wrong rate is how the exciting project gets approved. The exciting project is the one somebody loves. The dull project can survive a harsh rate and should be shown surviving it. The exciting project should be shown failing a harsh rate, if it fails, while there is still time to love it less.
