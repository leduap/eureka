# 3. Debt is a promise with a fence around it

Sabine Keller comes in with coffee and a term sheet. The house bank will lend another €6,000,000, at 5.5 percent, ten years, to pay for the calibration line and to leave something over. She is not pushing. She has known Klaus since the firm had one milling machine. She is telling them the money is there.

Klaus has never liked debt. The firm owes €8,000,000 at 5 percent. Interest is €400,000. Operating profit is €6,000,000. The interest could be paid fifteen times over. He would like it to be payable a hundred times over, which is another way of saying he would like it to be zero.

Martin asks Leonie for the course answer, and then for the answer she would give his father.

## What the course says, with the tax and without it

Without tax, the course has a famous result. If the cash the firm produces does not depend on how it is split between bank and family, the split does not change what the firm is worth. The family gives up a safer claim and keeps a riskier one. The risks add back to the same pie. Borrowing is not magic.

With tax, interest is deductible and a dividend is not. Debt becomes a shield. At the teaching rate of 30 percent, every euro of debt that stays forever is worth 30 cents of shield, if there will always be profit to shield and the debt will always be there. On €8,000,000 that is €2,400,000 of value. The course writes:

value with debt = value with no debt + tax rate × debt

Leonie computes the no-debt value at the asset rate, which is 3 percent + 0.63 × 5 percent = 6.16 percent. Cash after tax, in a steady year where new machines cost the same as depreciation, is €4,200,000. Value with no debt is €4,200,000 / 0.0616 = about **€68,200,000**. Add the shield and the course says **€70,600,000**.

**Write this down.** Do you add that €2,400,000 on top of a valuation you already did at the 6 percent weighted average?

No. The 6 percent is already cheaper because the debt's tax shield was folded into it. Add the shield again and you have counted Sabine twice. This is the most expensive double count in the course. People do it in job interviews with a straight face.

## The fence

The term sheet is one page. Two lines do the work.

- Net debt divided by EBITDA will not go above 2.0.
- Operating profit divided by interest will not go below 4.0.

EBITDA is operating profit plus depreciation, €7,200,000. Net debt today is €8,000,000 minus cash of €2,400,000, so €5,600,000. The ratio is 0.78. Interest cover is 15. Klaus is right that the firm is safe. He is wrong if he thinks the new loan keeps it that way in a bad year.

Take the €6,000,000. Debt becomes €14,000,000. Interest becomes €400,000 on the old loan plus €330,000 on the new one, so €730,000.

In a normal year the fence holds. Net debt is €11,600,000. Divided by €7,200,000 is 1.61. Cover is €6,000,000 / €730,000 = 8.2. Sabine would be pleased. So would the course.

Now the year the workshops stop buying. EBITDA falls to €4,000,000. Depreciation does not fall. It is a spreadsheet entry, not a tap. Operating profit is €2,800,000.

**Write this down.** Net debt over EBITDA, and interest cover, in that year. Does the fence hold?

Net debt is still €11,600,000 if nobody has repaid and the cash pile is unchanged. €11,600,000 / €4,000,000 = **2.90**. The maximum was 2.0.

Cover is €2,800,000 / €730,000 = **3.84**. The minimum was 4.0.

Both lines break. Not because the firm is bankrupt. Because a promise written in a good year is still a promise in a bad one.

What the bank can do next is not in the formula. It can raise the rate, ask for a repayment, or sit down with Klaus, whom Sabine has known for twenty years, and rewrite the page. A Mittelstand loan is a contract plus a relationship. The course examines the contract. The relationship is why the contract sometimes survives. You do not get marks for the relationship. You also do not get to ignore it if you work here.

## Three hands

Jonas says: take the €6,000,000. The tax shield is €1,800,000 on the new debt alone, the fair-weather ratios are fine, and "unused debt capacity" is the phrase he underlines.

Amina says: take less than they offer. Size the loan so the bad year still clears 2.0 and 4.0. Work backwards from €4,000,000 of EBITDA.

Piotr says: ask who believes EBITDA can fall to €4,000,000, and ask it of Klaus, not of the spreadsheet. If Klaus thinks that year is fantasy, the fence is cheap. If Klaus lived through that year in 2009 and does not want to live through the phone call, the shield is not worth the sleep.

Amina's arithmetic is the Monday work. In a €4,000,000 EBITDA year, net debt of 2.0 times is €8,000,000. Cash is €2,400,000, so gross debt can be about €10,400,000 before the first ratio breaks, and only if cash does not fall too, which in that year it will. The second ratio breaks earlier: operating profit of €2,800,000 divided by 4 is €700,000 of interest the fence allows. The old loan already costs €400,000. The new loan at 5.5 percent can be a little over €5,000,000 before cover in the bad year is 4.0, and that is before cash starts to drop. The offer of €6,000,000 is a good-year offer.

Piotr has the better question. A fence you set without the person who has seen a bad year is a fence drawn by someone who has only seen the case.

The Monday rule. **Take the tax shield. Then write the bad year next to the covenant, and size the debt to the bad year, not to the offer.**

## Where the case goes quiet

The shield assumes the debt is permanent and the profit is permanent. A loan that is paid down over ten years is a smaller shield than 30 percent times the opening balance. The course often uses the permanent version because it fits on one line. Say "permanent" when you use it.

Personal guarantees are not in the case either. In a firm this size they are sometimes on the back page, in Klaus's name. A guarantee moves the risk from the pie to a person. Modigliani and Miller were not talking about a person.

## Check

1. Do not add €2,400,000 of tax shield to a value you computed at the 6 percent weighted average. It is already in there.
2. With the extra €6,000,000, a normal year still clears the fence: net debt over EBITDA 1.61, cover 8.2.
3. A €4,000,000 EBITDA year breaks both lines: 2.90 and 3.84.
4. Interest on the combined debt is €730,000, not €14,000,000 times 5.5 percent. The old €8,000,000 stays at 5 percent.

## Exhibit 3. The fence in two years

| Cash, if it holds | 2,400,000 | 2,400,000 |

Interest is not €14,000,000 × 5.5 percent. The old €8,000,000 stays at 5 percent, which is €400,000. The new €6,000,000 at 5.5 percent is €330,000. Together, €730,000. Write the two loans on two lines or you will invent a rate the bank did not offer.

## Size it backwards

Amina's question can be finished with a pencil. In the bad year, operating profit is €2,800,000. Cover of 4 allows interest of €2,800,000 / 4 = **€700,000**. The old loan already costs €400,000. Room for new interest is €300,000. At 5.5 percent that is **€5,454,545** of new debt, and only if the cash pile does not shrink in the same year. The offer of €6,000,000 is past that room. A loan of €5,000,000 would leave a little air. Show Klaus both, and let him pick the air.

**Write this down.** If cash also falls by €1,000,000 in the bad year, what happens to net debt, before you even talk about cover?

It rises by €1,000,000, to €12,600,000. The ratio gets worse than 2.90. Cash is part of the fence. A forecast that holds cash flat in a bad year is a gentle forecast.

## The pie, without the tax, said in the room

Klaus's dislike of debt is older than the course, and the course has a famous result that seems to scold him. If the cash the firm produces does not depend on how it is split between the bank and the family, the split does not change what the firm is worth. Borrow more, and the family keeps a riskier slice of the same pie. Borrow less, and the family keeps a safer slice of the same pie. The slices add back. Borrowing is not a machine for making value. It is a machine for moving risk onto a particular person, and for moving cash from one pocket to another.

This is the result people quote when they want a meeting to end. It is true in a world with no taxes, no costs of going broke, and no Sabine who can ask for the money back on a Tuesday. Hartfeld does not live in that world. Use the result as a warning against magic, not as a permission to ignore the term sheet. The magic to be warned against is the sentence "debt is cheaper, so more debt makes us richer" with no mention of whose claim got riskier. The bank's 5 percent looks cheaper than the family's 7.5 percent because the bank gets paid first. The family is paid what is left. You cannot keep the cheapness and also keep the family's old safety. That trade is the whole of the no-tax theory, and it is the part Klaus already understands in his stomach. He does not need a theorem to know that a loan is a promise. He needs the theorem so that Martin cannot tell him the promise is free.

## The shield, counted once

Tax is what makes the promise look free, and it is also what makes people count it twice.

Interest is deductible. A dividend is not. At the teaching rate of 30 percent, a euro of interest saves 30 cents of tax, if there is profit to save it from. A euro of debt that stays forever, and is forever sheltered by profit, is worth 30 cents of value today, because the shield is a perpetuity of tax saved and the arithmetic of a perpetuity at the debt rate collapses, in the textbook, to tax rate times debt. On the €8,000,000 already borrowed, that is **€2,400,000**. The course writes a one-line firm:

value with debt = value with no debt + tax rate × debt

Leonie's no-debt value uses the asset rate, 3 percent plus 0.63 times 5 percent, which is 6.16 percent. Steady cash of €4,200,000 divided by 0.0616 is about **€68,200,000**. Add the shield and the course says about **€70,600,000**. The ledger's unrounded pair is €68,186,528 and €70,586,528. The meeting can live with €68.2 million and €70.6 million. It cannot live with someone adding €2,400,000 on top of a value they have already computed at the 6 percent weighted average.

The 6 percent is already cheaper because the debt's tax shield was folded into it. That was the move from 5 percent to 3.5 percent in chapter 2. Add the shield again and Sabine has been invited twice. This is the most expensive double count in the course, and it is expensive precisely because both halves look literate. A person who knows the one-line formula and a person who knows the weighted average can each be right in their own chapter and wrong in the same spreadsheet. The rule is about sequence. Pick one home for the shield and stay there. If you value at the weighted average, the shield is inside the rate, and you do not add it at the end. If you value at the asset rate, as if the firm had no debt, you may add the shield at the end, once, and you say "permanent" when you do it.

A loan that pays down over ten years is a smaller shield than 30 percent of the opening balance, because the balance does not stay. The course uses the permanent version because it fits on one line and because the examination can mark a line. Say the word permanent. If the real loan amortises, the shield is a stream you discount, not a lump you memorised. The direction of the mistake, if you use the lump on a loan that disappears, is to overstate the value of borrowing. Klaus's caution, aimed at that mistake, is more accurate than Martin's enthusiasm.

## The two lines that do the work

The term sheet is one page so that the rest of the page can look like hospitality. Two lines are the fence.

Net debt divided by EBITDA will not go above 2.0. Operating profit divided by interest will not go below 4.0.

Today, before the new money, net debt is €5,600,000 and EBITDA is €7,200,000. The ratio is 0.78. Interest cover is 15. Klaus is right about the firm he has. He is being offered a firm he has not got.

Take the €6,000,000. Debt becomes €14,000,000. Do not multiply €14,000,000 by 5.5 percent. The old €8,000,000 stays at 5 percent, which is €400,000. The new money at 5.5 percent is €330,000. Interest is **€730,000**. Write the loans on two lines. A blended rate is a way of forgetting that the bank did not reprice the old loan, and also a way of inventing a rate nobody offered.

In a year that looks like the year just closed, net debt is €11,600,000 if cash stays €2,400,000. Divided by €7,200,000 that is 1.61. Cover is €6,000,000 / €730,000, which is 8.2. The fence holds. Sabine would be pleased. The course would be pleased. Both of them are looking at a year that has already happened, plus a loan that has not yet met a bad one.

Now the workshops stop buying. EBITDA falls to €4,000,000. Depreciation does not fall with it. It is the spreading of machines you already own. Operating profit is €2,800,000. Net debt, if cash does not fall either, is still €11,600,000. The ratio is **2.90**. Cover is **3.84**. The maximum was 2.0. The minimum was 4.0. Both lines break. The firm is not bankrupt in this paragraph. It is in breach. Breach is a legal fact and a phone call. The formula ends before the phone call. Your job does not.

What the bank can do next is not in the textbook's one line. It can raise the rate, ask for money back, tighten the next fence, or sit with Klaus, whom Sabine has known since the firm had one milling machine, and rewrite the page. A Mittelstand loan is a contract plus a relationship. You do not get marks for the relationship. You do not get to ignore it if the wages depend on it. The grown-up use of the course is to know the contract well enough that the relationship is not the only thing standing between February and a surprise.

## Sizing it from the bad year, in the order Amina meant

Work backwards, and do not skip the cash.

In the bad year, operating profit is €2,800,000. Cover of 4 allows interest of €700,000. The old loan already costs €400,000. Room for new interest is **€300,000**. At 5.5 percent that is **€5,454,545** of new debt, and only if the cash pile does not shrink. The offer of €6,000,000 is past that room. A loan of €5,000,000 would leave a little air. Show Klaus both numbers and let him buy the air. "A little over €5,000,000" is the sentence for the room. €5,454,545 is the sentence for the page, so that nobody rounds it back up to the offer.

The other ratio is less kind once cash moves. Net debt of 2.0 times €4,000,000 of EBITDA is €8,000,000. Cash of €2,400,000 would then allow gross debt of about €10,400,000, which looks more generous than the cover test. It is more generous only while cash holds. If cash falls by €1,000,000 in the same bad year, net debt on the €14,000,000 loan rises to €12,600,000, and the ratio is worse than 2.90. A forecast that holds cash flat while the workshops stop buying is a gentle forecast. Gentle forecasts are how fences get signed. Write the cash drop on the same page as the EBITDA drop, or you have stress-tested the profit and assumed the bank account is a different firm.

Jonas's "unused debt capacity" is the gap between 0.78 and 2.0, read in a good year. Capacity that exists only in a good year is not capacity. It is weather. The Monday work is to compute the loan that still fits when the weather the founder actually remembers has been written down. If Klaus says the €4,000,000 year is a fantasy, the fence is cheap and the shield is a gift. If Klaus lived through that phone call and does not want it again, the shield is a bad price for sleep. Ask him. Do not ask the spreadsheet. The spreadsheet has never met 2009.

## The name on the back page

Personal guarantees are the silence in the famous result. In a firm this size they sometimes sit on the back of the term sheet, in Klaus's name, or in Martin's, or in both. A guarantee moves the risk from the pie to a person. The theorem was about claims on a firm. It was not about a house. If the bad year breaks the fence and the guarantee is real, the family's safety is not the residual claim in a model. It is a person who cannot sleep. You will not be asked to price a guarantee in this course. You will be asked, by the actual term sheet, whether one is there. Read the back page before you congratulate the room on the tax shield.

Two more sentences belong in the file, because they stop the shield being spent twice. First: we are not adding €2,400,000 to a 6 percent valuation. Second: the new loan is sized to the bad year's cover, at €5,454,545 of room, and the offer is €6,000,000. The relationship with Sabine is a reason the breach might be survivable. It is not a reason to sign a breach in advance.

## How the course marks the fence

The examination wants the one-line formula and the refusal to double-count. The job wants the bad year on the same sheet as the offer. Full marks here are both. A beautiful €70.6 million with no covenant is a finance answer and an incomplete memo. A covenant with no understanding of the shield is a banking answer that will lose the argument to Martin, who will say the tax makes the loan wise and will not show the year the wisdom expires.

Write the bad year in the same ink as the offer. If the page has the offer in ink and the bad year in a spoken aside, the minute will record the offer.

## In the room

The question is the debt-policy question. How much is enough, once tax makes debt look free? The page the case leaves out is the covenant in the year the sales are not there, and the person whose name is on the relationship.
