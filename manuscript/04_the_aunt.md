# 4. Cash can leave as a dividend or as a purchase of shares

Ingrid asks for a meeting and does not sit down. She is sixty-eight. She owns 20 percent. She would like €1,200,000 paid out this spring. She has a house to fix and a habit of not explaining the house.

Profit after tax was €3,920,000. Cash is €2,400,000. A payout of €1,200,000 is 31 percent of the profit. It is also half the cash.

Martin says, "The course prefers a buyback." He heard that in a lecture and he has been waiting to use it.

Leonie asks which course. The lecture was about listed shares in a country where a buyback and a dividend are taxed differently and the shares trade every afternoon. Hartfeld is a German private company. The shares do not trade every afternoon. They barely trade at all.

Do the lecture anyway. Then say what does not cross the border.

## The lecture, on a firm that is not quite this one

Give Hartfeld, just for this chapter, a million imaginary shares and a price taken from the peer you will meet properly in chapter 6. Eight times operating profit is €48,000,000 for the business. Take off net debt of €5,600,000. The equity is €42,400,000. Each imaginary share is **€42.40**. Ingrid's 20 percent is €8,480,000 on that story. It is not an offer. It is a ruler.

A dividend of €1,200,000 pays her 20 percent, which is **€240,000** gross.

If that €240,000 were taxed as private investment income in Germany, the flat tax is 25 percent and the solidarity surcharge is 5.5 percent of the tax, so 26.375 percent together. Tax is €63,300. She keeps **€176,700**. A holder of 20 percent is often not in that simple box. Write the gross figure in ink and the tax figure in pencil, and let her adviser rub out the pencil.

A buyback of €1,200,000 at €42.40 retires 28,302 shares.

**Write this down.** If Ingrid is the only seller, what fraction does she own afterwards? If everyone sells in proportion, what fraction does she own afterwards?

If she is the only seller she sells 28,302 of her 200,000 shares. The firm then has 971,698 shares. She has 171,698 of them. Her fraction falls from 20 percent to **17.7 percent**. She has cash, and she has less of the firm.

If everyone sells in proportion, she sells a fifth of the shares the firm buys, receives the same €240,000, and still owns 20 percent of a slightly smaller firm. The cash in her hand matches the dividend. The stake matches the morning she walked in.

That is the whole lecture. On a listed company, with a price, a pro-rata buyback and a dividend are the same pie cut with a different knife. The knife matters for tax and for anyone who does not take part. It does not matter for the size of the pie. Paying cash out does not make the family richer in total. It moves wealth from the firm's account to a person's account. Klaus, who was going to leave his shares to Martin anyway, may not want the move. Ingrid, who wants a roof, does.

## What the lecture is doing inside Hartfeld

Cash is €2,400,000. After a €1,200,000 payout it is €1,200,000. The calibration line, if they buy it, wants €2,700,000 before any customer pays. You have just met chapter 1 in a worse mood.

**Write this down.** Can the firm pay Ingrid and sign for the line in the same month, without Sabine?

No. The line and the parts need €2,700,000. The payout would leave €1,200,000. The gap is €1,500,000, and that is before wages. The course separates payout policy from capital budgeting because the textbook has two chapters. The firm has one bank account.

The grown-up sequence is dull and correct. Decide the line. Decide the month the cash is lowest. Then see what can be paid to Ingrid without making that month a phone call to the bank. If the answer is €400,000 this spring and €800,000 after the first customers pay, say that. A roof can be phased. An overdraft covenant is less patient.

## Three hands

Jonas says: buy back her shares. "Dividends signal. A one-off buyback does not reset expectations." He has brought a listed-company habit into a family that does not have a share price.

Amina says: pay the dividend, and show Klaus the €1,200,000 beside the line's €2,700,000 on one page, so the fight is about the calendar and not about Ingrid's character.

Piotr says: ask Ingrid whether she wants to be smaller in the firm. If she wants cash and the same 20 percent, it is a dividend, or a pro-rata purchase the two men will hate, because they have to find cash too. If she wants out, it is a purchase of her shares at a price the three of them will argue about for a year, and chapter 6 is that argument.

Amina has the page that should go to the meeting. Piotr has the question that should open it. Jonas has a sentence from the wrong country.

The Monday rule. **Pay out what the lowest cash month can spare. Do not let a lecture about buybacks spend the money the machines need.**

## Where the case goes quiet

A German private company does not buy its own shares the way a listed American company does. There is a notary in the sentence, and there are rules about not paying out the capital the creditors rely on. None of that is in the lecture Martin remembers. The blue page can still be true: cash to owners is cash not in the firm. The paperwork is a different chapter, and it belongs to a lawyer.

The other silence is the price. €42.40 is eight times operating profit, borrowed from a listed peer. If Ingrid sells at that price and the peer was the wrong ruler, someone at Sunday lunch has been cheated. Chapter 6 is where the ruler gets examined. Do not let this chapter settle her exit.

## Check

1. Her gross dividend is €240,000. At 26.375 percent she keeps €176,700, and that rate is a headline, not her assessment.
2. If she is the only seller at €42.40, her stake falls from 20 percent to about 17.7 percent.
3. If everyone sells in proportion, her stake stays 20 percent and her cash matches the dividend.
4. The firm cannot pay her €1,200,000 and buy the line in the same breath. The line wants €2,700,000. The cash, after her cheque, is €1,200,000.

## In the room

The question is the payout question. Dividend or buyback, and does the pie change? The page the case leaves out is the other use of the same cash, sitting in the next folder, and the law of the country the firm is actually in.
