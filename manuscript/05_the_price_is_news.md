# 5. A price is already a piece of news

Nordmess AG is the listed company people mention when they mention Hartfeld. It makes measuring equipment too, with more factories and a ticker. This week a trade paper says Nordmess has lost a French contract. The shares fall 9 percent before Leonie has finished the article.

Martin is pleased, in a way he will not say at dinner. "If we were buyers, that is cheaper. Is the course telling me to wait for bad headlines?"

No. The course is telling him the opposite.

## The price moved because the news was news

A share price is a meeting place. The people who think Nordmess is worth more and the people who think it is worth less have already shouted at each other, and the last shout is the price. When a French contract is lost, the people who had not counted on that contract change their mind, and the price drops by about the size of the surprise.

The drop is not a sale. It is the market subtracting something it had been adding.

**Write this down.** Nordmess falls 9 percent on the headline. You read the same headline an hour later. Do you have an advantage?

You do not. The hour was enough. By the time a story is a story, it is in the price. The course calls this the efficient market, and then spends a week telling you the ways it is not quite true. For a person sitting in Munich with a trade paper and no special access, it is true enough. You will not beat Nordmess by being a careful reader of the trade paper. Careful readers are the market.

What would be an advantage is dull and rare. A fact you have a right to know and others do not, which inside a firm is called your job and outside a firm is sometimes called a crime. Hartfeld's own order book is that kind of fact about Hartfeld. It is not that kind of fact about Nordmess.

## The index does not get to be clever

There is a second machine in this chapter, and it does not read the trade paper at all.

Suppose a new company joins the index that a pension fund has promised to track. The fund does not ask whether the price is wise. The promise was to own what the index owns. The fund buys. If the index adds the company on the day it lists, the fund buys on that day, at that day's price, with every other fund that made the same promise.

Leonie writes the sentence she wants Martin to see. **An index fund is a buyer who is not allowed to wait.**

That is not a criticism of index funds. It is the specification. The cost of being average is that you pay the price of the day the index changes. The alternative is a manager who waits, charges more, and may still be wrong. Chapter 2 already picked a side for the family's idle money: do not pretend to be that manager. This chapter only adds the bill. You will own the expensive new listing if your fund tracks an index that includes it immediately. Some indexes wait. The waiting is the product. Read which one you bought.

Martin's question about Nordmess is the same machine, run backwards. The price fell because the contract left. Waiting for bad headlines, as a strategy, means you believe the headline overstates the damage and that you are early. You are not early. You are holding a newspaper.

## Three hands

Jonas says: buy Nordmess this afternoon. "Nine percent is a dislocation. Dislocations revert."

Amina says: do nothing, and write down why. If Hartfeld one day buys a company, the price you pay has to be justified by Hartfeld's own savings, not by a red day on a screen.

Piotr says: he would call the salesperson at Nordmess and ask whether the French work is the sort of work Hartfeld could do. The share price is not his lead. A customer with a hole in a supply contract might be.

Jonas is trading a headline. Amina is protecting a future negotiation. Piotr is doing commercial work and calling it finance, which is allowed. None of the three has a reason to think 9 percent was the wrong amount.

The Monday rule. **If you learned it from a paper, the price learned it first.**

## Where the case goes quiet

Prices are not wise about things nobody knows, and they are not wise about things a seller is hiding. They are also jumpy in the hour of the headline and calmer a week later, sometimes. A course that says "you cannot beat the market" and a course that says "the market overreacts for a week" are both taught, often by the same department. The usable rule for Leonie is narrower. She has no edge on Nordmess. She has an edge on whether Hartfeld's calibration line earns €690,000 a year, and even that edge expires the day the customers do something else.

The other silence is Hartfeld itself. There is no ticker. Klaus cannot look up whether the family is wrong. Chapter 6 is the substitute, and it will disagree with itself, on purpose.

## Check

1. A 9 percent fall on a public lost contract is the market changing its mind, not a coupon.
2. Reading the same article later is not an advantage.
3. A fund that tracks an index buys a new member because it promised to, at the price of that day.

The 9 percent is not a number you check. It is a number you refuse to trade on. The useful question is commercial. A customer in France may now be short of tools. That is Piotr's phone call, and it does not require you to own Nordmess.

## The index, in four lines

1. A fund promises to own what the index owns.
2. The index adds a new company.
3. The fund buys, that day, at that price, with every other fund that made the same promise.
4. Waiting for a better price would break the promise. The price of the promise is sometimes a high one.

**Write this down.** Your own savings, if they sit in a fund that tracks an index on the day of entry, will buy that new company. Is that a reason to leave the fund?

Not by itself. It is a reason to know which index you bought, and whether it waits before a new listing goes in. The cost of being average includes this bill. The cost of not being average is a manager, a fee, and a fresh chance to be wrong. Chapter 2 already chose, for money the family is not using in the firm. This chapter only tells you to read the rule you already accepted.

## What the price is a meeting of

A share price is not an opinion handed down. It is the last price at which a buyer and a seller agreed to stop arguing. Everyone who thought Nordmess was worth more has either bought or decided the price had already passed them. Everyone who thought it was worth less has either sold or decided to live with it. The French contract, once it is in a trade paper, is no longer a secret sitting in a factory. It is a reason for those people to change their minds, and the 9 percent is the size of the change, argued out by the people who do this for a living, in the time it took Leonie to finish the article.

That is a humbling description, and it is the one the course wants in the first week of markets, before it spends the next week showing you the holes. The holes are real. Prices jump. Prices sulk. A stock can fall 9 percent and then another 4 by Friday because the first sellers were not finished, or because the contract was worse than the headline. A person in Munich with a trade paper is not the one who will know that in the hour. The people who might know it already trade the stock, or they are inside Nordmess, where knowing it and trading it is a different chapter of the law. Hartfeld's own order book is the kind of fact Leonie is paid to know about Hartfeld. It is not a fact about Nordmess. Confusing the two is how a finance office starts to think of itself as a fund.

Martin's pleasure at the red day is the pleasure of a shopper. Cheap, in a shop, means the seller has not yet heard the news you have heard. Cheap, in a share that fell on a public headline, means the news has been subtracted. You are not early. You are holding a newspaper. Waiting for bad headlines, as a strategy, means you believe the headline overstates the damage and that the market will come round to your calmer view. Sometimes it does. You do not have a reason, this afternoon, to think you are the calm one and the market is the panicked one. You have a reason to think you read more slowly.

Write the distinction in a sentence he can repeat at dinner without embarrassing himself. The drop is not a sale. It is the market taking back something it had been adding. If Hartfeld one day buys a company, the price has to be justified by the cash Hartfeld can earn with it, including any saving that comes from being one firm instead of two. A red day is not that justification. It is the seller's morning. Using it as yours is how buyers overpay with a feeling of thrift.

## The buyer who is not allowed to wait

There is a second machine in the chapter, and it does not read the trade paper.

A pension fund has promised to own what an index owns. A new company joins the index. The fund does not ask whether the price is wise. The promise was the product. The fund buys, that day, at that day's price, beside every other fund that made the same promise. If the addition is large and the funds are large, the buying itself is part of the price. That is not a scandal and it is not a trick you are clever enough to stand in front of. It is the specification. The cost of being average includes this bill. Some indexes wait before a new listing goes in. The waiting is a different product. Read which one you bought before you complain about the bill.

Martin's question about Nordmess is the same machine run backwards. He wants to be the person who waits. An index fund is the person who is not allowed to. Neither of them, in this chapter, has an edge. The fund's lack of edge is honest: it said so in the prospectus. Martin's hoped-for edge is a mood. Moods are allowed at dinner. They are not a reason to spend the firm's cash, or the family's savings, on a listed share that fell for a reason you can read.

Chapter 2 already picked a side for money the family is not using in the machines. Do not pretend to be a manager who waits, charges more, and may still be wrong. This chapter only adds the bill that comes with the other choice. You will own the expensive new listing if your fund tracks an index that includes it immediately. You will also own Nordmess on the red day and on the green one, in whatever weight the index gives it, without having to decide whether 9 percent was the right amount. That is the point of the product. Leaving the fund because of one red day is a way of becoming the manager you declined to hire, without the manager's staff, and with your own evening as the research department.

## What would actually be an edge

The course spends a week on the ways markets are not quite efficient, and a fair session should not pretend the week does not exist. Prices are not wise about things nobody knows. They are not wise about a seller who is hiding a fact. They are jumpy in the hour of a headline and sometimes calmer a week later. A department can teach "you cannot beat the market" on Monday and "the market overreacts for a week" on Thursday without the teachers feeling they have contradicted themselves. They have narrowed the claim. The usable rule for Leonie is narrower still.

She has no edge on Nordmess. She might, in time, have an edge on whether Hartfeld's calibration line earns €690,000 a year, and even that edge expires the day the customers do something else. She might have an edge on a French workshop that has just lost a supplier and needs tools. That edge is commercial. It is a phone call. It does not require Hartfeld to own a single share of Nordmess, and it does not become more intelligent because the shares fell. Piotr's instinct is the one that belongs in a firm that makes things. Jonas's instinct belongs in a trading floor, where the job is the dislocation and the risk is that the dislocation continues. Amina's instinct belongs in a future negotiation, if Hartfeld ever buys someone: write down why the price is justified by Hartfeld's own cash, so that a red screen cannot do the persuading.

None of the three has a reason to think 9 percent was the wrong amount. Hold that. The chapter is not a claim that markets are perfect. It is a claim that a trade paper in Munich is a bad place to look for a bargain in a stock that has already moved.

## What she puts in the file

The file is almost empty, which is the achievement.

Nordmess fell 9 percent on a public lost contract. We have no edge an hour later. We do not buy, and we do not wait in order to buy. If a salesperson wants to call on the French work the contract left behind, that is a sales question, and it does not need a share trade attached to it.

The family's money outside the firm, if it sits in an index fund, will buy what the index adds, on the day the index adds it. That is a reason to know which index it is. It is not, by itself, a reason to leave. The cost of not being average is a manager, a fee, and a fresh chance to be wrong.

There is no ticker on Hartfeld. This chapter does not tell us what the family is worth. It tells us not to borrow Nordmess's afternoon as if it were a valuation of our own firm. Chapter 6 is the substitute, and it will disagree with itself on purpose.

## How the course marks a newspaper

A student who buys the dip because "nine percent always comes back" has made a forecast and called it a principle. Sometimes dips come back. The mark is for noticing that you needed a reason you had and the market did not, and that the trade paper did not give you one. A student who refuses all commercial action because "the market is efficient" has over-read the chapter. The market in shares can be a hard room to outsmart while a customer in France is still short of tools. Separate the two sentences in the answer, or the marker will think you cannot tell a share from a workshop.

## In the room

The question is the market-efficiency question. What is already in the price? The page the case leaves out is the buyer who is obliged to pay it.
