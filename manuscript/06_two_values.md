# 6. A firm can have two values before lunch

Klaus asks a question he has avoided for a decade. "If Ingrid sold, what is the firm worth? One number. I am tired of ranges."

Leonie brings two, and refuses to average them.

## The steady cash

In a year that looks like the year just closed, operating profit is €6,000,000. Tax at 30 percent is €1,800,000. What remains is €4,200,000.

Depreciation was €1,200,000. If the firm spends the same €1,200,000 on machines just to stay the same size, that spending cancels the depreciation you added back. Customers and inventory, if they do not grow, swallow no extra cash. The cash you could hand to the bank and the family, together, is €4,200,000 a year. The course calls this free cash flow to the firm. The name is grander than the arithmetic.

At 6 percent, a stream of €4,200,000 that never grows and never shrinks is worth

€4,200,000 / 0.06 = **€70,000,000**.

That is the value of the business, not of the shares. The bank has a claim. Net debt is €5,600,000. The family's claim is **€64,400,000**.

If you used the unrounded 5.99 percent you would be about €110,000 higher on the business. Do not lose a Sunday to it.

## The peer

Nordmess is talked about at eight times operating profit. The multiple is a rumour of a market, not a printout Leonie should pretend she traded. Use it as the case uses it: a ruler someone in the room believes.

Eight times €6,000,000 is **€48,000,000** for the business. Minus €5,600,000 of net debt, the family would be worth **€42,400,000**. Ingrid's 20 percent, on this ruler, is €8,480,000. This is the price behind the €42.40 share in chapter 4.

**Write this down.** Why are €70,000,000 and €48,000,000 both able to walk into the same meeting?

Because they are answers to different questions.

€70,000,000 says: the next many years look like last year, and a euro of that cash is worth six cents of return, forever.

€48,000,000 says: a listed peer is priced at eight times operating profit, and Hartfeld is the same kind of thing.

Divide €4,200,000 by €48,000,000 and you get 8.75 percent. The peer's ruler is behaving like a buyer who wants **8.75 percent** on the same steady cash, not 6. Or it is behaving like a buyer who does not believe the €4,200,000 will last. You cannot tell which from the multiple alone. That is why averaging the two values, and telling Klaus €59,000,000, is a number nobody in the room actually believed.

## Which one you say out loud

For the calibration line, 6 percent was enough. The line is a small bet on the firm's own factory. The risk matches the rate, near enough, and the alternative was Martin's 8 percent, which the line still cleared.

For Ingrid's shares, the peer matters, because a buyer who is not in the family will think like a buyer of Nordmess, and will also want a discount for the fact that he cannot sell on Monday. A private share is not a listed share. The €42,400,000 is already a listed-style ruler. A buyer of a 20 percent private stake often pays less again, because he will be stuck with Klaus. The course calls this a discount for a share that does not trade. The Sunday lunch calls it not getting pushed around.

**Your number.** Klaus wants one number for Ingrid's 20 percent. Give him a sentence instead of a compromise. Fill in the blanks.

"On last year's cash, at 6 percent, 20 percent of the equity is about ______. On the peer's multiple, 20 percent is about ______. A buyer who cannot sell on Monday will try to pay less than both."

Twenty percent of €64,400,000 is **€12,880,000**. Twenty percent of €42,400,000 is **€8,480,000**. The distance between them is the argument. Collapsing it is how families discover, five years later, that one of them feels robbed.

## Three hands

Jonas says: use the peer. "Cash is a theory. Multiples are what people pay."

Amina says: use the cash, and show the peer as a cross-check. If they are far apart, the work is to explain the gap, not to hide it.

Piotr says: ask what the cash is after the line, and after Ingrid is paid, and after a bad year. "Last year is not a promise. You have valued a photograph."

Amina is right about the page. Piotr is right about the photograph. Jonas is right that a buyer arrives with a multiple and a lower offer. Let Jonas play the buyer in the meeting. Do not let him write the minutes.

The Monday rule. **Two values are a finding. The average of two values is a cover-up.**

## Where the case goes quiet

No growth. The €70,000,000 assumes Hartfeld never sells more than €40,000,000 and never needs extra inventory to do it. A firm that grows will have more cash later and less cash now, because growth eats parts and customer credit. Growing can reduce the value you can hold in your hand this decade. The course has a formula for that. It can wait until someone claims the firm is about to double. Nobody in this family is claiming that, except the engineer with the gauge, and chapter 2 already priced his optimism.

The other silence is the bank. €5,600,000 of net debt comes off the end. If the debt is cheap and long, subtracting it euro for euro is fair. If Sabine can ask for it back when the ratios break, the debt is shorter than it looks, and the family's claim is riskier than a subtraction suggests. Chapter 3 was that fence. Bring it into the room when the value is for a sale. Leave it outside when the value is for a machine.

## Check

1. Steady cash to the firm's capital is €4,200,000. At 6 percent forever, the business is €70,000,000. Equity is €64,400,000.
2. At eight times operating profit the business is €48,000,000. Equity is €42,400,000.
3. The gap is an 8.75 percent buyer versus a 6 percent buyer, or a doubt that the cash lasts. It is not a suggestion to say €59,000,000.
4. Ingrid's 20 percent is €12,880,000 on the cash and €8,480,000 on the multiple, before any discount for being unable to sell.

€4,200,000 / €48,000,000 = 8.75 percent. The multiple is what you would pay if you wanted 8.75 percent on the same steady cash, or if you did not believe the cash would stay. Write that sentence under the table. If you cannot write it, you do not yet know why the two numbers differ, and you are not ready to speak to Klaus.

## A buyer's discount, said plainly

Both numbers above pretend a buyer can sell on Monday. Ingrid's buyer cannot. He will be a minority next to Klaus, in a company with no ticker and a house bank that takes Klaus's calls. He will ask for a discount. The course is right to give it a name. The meeting is right to argue the size. Ten percent is a mood. Thirty percent is a different deal. Do not pick one in the appendix and call it science. Pick one in the room, and write down what would change it: a path for him to be bought out later, a seat, a dividend policy that is not a surprise.

**Write this down.** At a 25 percent discount to the multiple, what is 20 percent worth? Do it on the €8,480,000, not on the €12,880,000, because a buyer who wants a discount is already standing on the peer, not on your hope.

€8,480,000 × 0.75 = **€6,360,000**. That is a number Ingrid can hate in a specific way, which is more useful than hating a range.

## In the room

The question is the valuation question. Cash versus a peer, and what you do when they disagree. The page the case leaves out is the photograph: last year, offered as if it were a promise.
