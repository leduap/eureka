# 7. Profit does not pay the supplier

In July a chain of workshops asks for a special run. €6,000,000 of tools, to their specification, delivery in a month. The cost of the goods is €3,600,000, the same 60 percent Hartfeld lives with. Gross profit is €2,400,000. Martin puts the percentage in the subject line of the email: 40 percent, like the firm, on a single order, from a name Klaus trusts.

Leonie builds the cash, not the percentage.

The supplier of the parts wants to be paid thirty days after Hartfeld orders them. The chain pays ninety days after delivery. The simplest calendar that respects both sentences is this.

- Day 0, Hartfeld orders the parts.
- Day 30, Hartfeld pays €3,600,000 and ships the tools.
- Day 120, the chain pays €6,000,000.

For ninety days the firm has paid for goods it has not been paid for.

**Write this down.** How much cash does the order tie up, between the payment to the supplier and the payment from the chain? And what does that cash cost, at the bank's 5 percent, for those ninety days?

The cash tied up is **€3,600,000**. Not the profit. The profit is still inside the customer's pocket.

Interest is €3,600,000 × 5 percent × 90 / 365 = **€44,384**.

The order is still a good order. €2,400,000 of gross profit minus €44,384 of carry is €2,355,616, before anyone allocates overhead or tax. The course is not telling you to refuse it. The course is telling you that "40 percent" did not answer the question Sabine will ask, which is whether the wages still go out in the ninety days when €3,600,000 is missing.

Cash on the balance sheet is €2,400,000. It does not cover €3,600,000. The firm needs the bank for the difference, about €1,200,000 at the bottom, and more if the ordinary customers pay late in the same month. Borrowing the whole €3,600,000 is cleaner. Debt would go from €8,000,000 to €11,600,000 for a season. Net debt would go from €5,600,000 to €9,200,000. Against EBITDA of €7,200,000 that is a ratio of about 1.3, still inside the fence of 2.0 from chapter 3. The order does not break the bank by itself. A second order of the same shape, taken before the first is paid, might.

## The days the firm already lives with

You can see the habit in the year just closed, without this order.

Customers take €5,000,000 / €40,000,000 × 365 = **46 days**.

Inventory sits for €4,800,000 / €24,000,000 × 365 = **73 days**.

Suppliers wait €3,000,000 / €24,000,000 × 365 = **46 days**.

Add the first two and subtract the third. The firm's cash is out for **73 days** in the ordinary course of making tools. The special order is that habit, stretched, because this customer wrote ninety days into the contract and the parts are not a standard shelf item. The stretch is a choice. It should be priced, or refused, on purpose.

**Your number.** Suppose the chain will accept sixty days if Hartfeld drops the price by €80,000. Do you take that?

The carry shortens by thirty days. Interest saved is €14,795, which is less than €80,000. On interest alone, you do not give up €80,000 to save thirty days.

You might give it up anyway, if the ninety days would stack on another late customer and push the fence, or if Klaus's rule is that no single customer may have more than €2,000,000 outstanding. €6,000,000 for ninety days breaks a rule the interest calculation does not know. The Monday question is which rule is real.

## Three hands

Jonas says: take the ninety days and borrow. "The spread is huge. You do not negotiate against yourself."

Amina says: offer sixty days for a €40,000 cut, not €80,000, because €40,000 is the interest plus a fee for the risk that a chain can be slow. If they refuse, take the ninety and tell Sabine before you tell the customer.

Piotr says: he wants the contract's late-payment line, and he wants to know whether the tools can be sold to anyone else if the chain cancels on day 25. Special specification means the €3,600,000 of parts is scrap if the chain walks. The interest is not the risk. The scrap is the risk.

Piotr has found the real downside. Amina has found the phone call. Jonas has found the gross margin and stopped looking, which is how the email got its subject line.

The Monday rule. **Price the days, and then ask what the goods are worth if the customer never collects them.**

## Where the case goes quiet

Overhead did not appear. The factory is busy. If this order pushes out ordinary work that would have paid faster, the 40 percent is being compared with the wrong alternative. The right alternative is the other use of the same month on the machines.

Tax did not appear either. The profit will be taxed. The cash for the tax may be due before the chain pays. Chapter 10 is that calendar. For a single order, look at it before you promise ninety days in a quarter when the tax office is also holding out its hand.

## The risk that is not the interest

Piotr's question survives every version of the interest arithmetic. The tools are to the chain's specification. If the chain cancels on day 25, after the parts are in the building and before they are a product anyone else can use, the €3,600,000 is not a receivable. It is scrap, or it is a long argument. The interest on a successful order is €44,384. The loss on a cancelled special is the parts. Those are not the same kind of number, and a room that has spent the hour on the €44,384 has spent it on the smaller one because the smaller one divides cleanly.

Ask for the late-payment line in the contract, and ask what the goods are worth to a different workshop. If the answer is "nothing, they are branded," then the order is an unsecured loan to the chain, with a manufacturing step in the middle, and the 40 percent is the interest on that loan only if they pay. Price it that way, or refuse it that way. Do not price it as if it were a repeat of last year's ordinary tools, which can be sold to the next name on the list.

Overhead did not appear, and it should be asked about even though it will not produce a new euro in this chapter. The factory is busy. The right alternative is the other use of the same month on the machines. A contribution that looks large against an empty factory looks ordinary against a full one. You do not have a second euro for that displaced work in the case. You have the duty to ask whether the factory is empty. A case that is silent is not a case that has answered.

Tax did not appear either. The profit will be taxed. The cash for the tax may be due before the chain pays. Chapter 10 is that calendar. For a single order, look at it before you promise ninety days in a quarter when the tax office is also holding out its hand. February's instalment does not care that the chain pays in ninety days. The two calendars are not in a negotiation with each other. You are the only person who can see both.
