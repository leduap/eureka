# 8. The number is made

It is the last week of the year. A workshop chain, a smaller one than July's, wants €900,000 of ordinary tools delivered on 28 December. The goods are on the shelf. They cost €540,000 to make. The customer will pay in February, which is normal. The salesperson wants the sale in this year. "The tools will be on their truck before New Year's Eve. That is a sale."

Martin looks at the profit. He is not a villain in this chapter. He would like the year to look like the year he promised Klaus in January. The promise was a photograph. December is where photographs are touched up.

## Book it, and book the dent

Delivered before midnight, with the customer obliged to pay, this is a sale of this year. Revenue €900,000. Cost €540,000. Gross profit **€360,000**. Cash in December: **zero**. Inventory falls. Customers-not-yet-paid rises. The profit is real in the way accountants mean real, and absent in the way Sabine means real.

Hartfeld's tools come back. Not often, but often enough that a silent year is a lie. Leonie uses 4 percent of the sale, because that is what the last three years of returns and repairs cost, and because a round number the firm will actually apply is better than a precise number it will forget. Four percent of €900,000 is **€36,000**.

Gross profit after that provision is **€324,000**. Still no cash.

**Write this down.** If the salesperson's version is booked, with no provision, by how many euros is this year's gross profit flattered?

**€36,000.** Small next to €16,000,000 of gross profit. Large next to a bonus, or a promise, that was missed by €20,000. The flattery is not in the truck. The flattery is in leaving out a cost you already know how to expect.

The provision is not cash either. It is a debt to the future, a guess with a name. Guessing is allowed. Guessing zero, when the last three years were not zero, is a choice.

## Who is paid on the photograph

The course spends this week on the statements as an input to every other week. The discount rate, the multiple, the covenant, the payout: all of them eat this profit. If the profit is a hope with the returns left out, chapter 6 valued a hope, and chapter 3 measured a fence against a hope.

**Your number.** Sabine asks one question in January. "How much of last year's profit had turned into cash by the morning of the board meeting?" You do not have a full cash-flow statement in this scene. What can you say, honestly, about this one sale?

You can say the €324,000 is a claim on a customer and a reserve for repairs, and that on the morning of a January meeting the cash from this truck has not arrived. If the meeting is early enough in February, it still has not arrived. Profit is a statement about the year. Cash is a statement about the day.

## Three hands

Jonas says: book the €900,000, skip the provision, and "clean it up next year if the returns actually happen." Moving a cost into a year where nobody is watching is an old trick. It is also how the next year's photograph starts already dirty.

Amina says: book the sale and the €36,000, and show both lines to Klaus, with the February cash date written beside them. The year can have the profit. The reader of the year has to see the timing.

Piotr says: he wants the salesperson's commission to be paid when the customer pays, not when the truck leaves. "You are arguing about the accounts. The accounts are behaving like the pay packet you wrote."

Piotr has the incentive. Amina has the entry. Jonas has a restatement waiting to happen.

The Monday rule. **If someone is paid when the number is booked, book the number as if the person you trust most were checking it.**

## Where the case goes quiet

Four percent is a policy, not a measurement from this customer. A new chain might return nothing, or might return everything. The case should say whose job it is to look at returns in March and change the 4 percent if it was a fairy tale. If nobody has that job, the provision is decoration.

The other silence is the audit, if there is one, and the tax office, which has its own view of when a sale is a sale. Chapter 10. A profit you book in December can be taxed before the customer pays. The touch-up has a bill.

## Check

1. The December sale can be this year's revenue. Cash in December is zero.
2. Gross profit before the provision is €360,000. The 4 percent provision is €36,000. Profit left is €324,000.
3. Leaving the provision out flatters gross profit by €36,000.
4. A covenant, a multiple, and a value built on this profit inherit the flattery.

## What "a sale" is, and what it is not

Delivered before midnight, with the customer obliged to pay, this is a sale of this year. That sentence is the accounting, and it is not a trick. Revenue €900,000. Cost of the tools €540,000. Gross profit **€360,000**. Inventory falls. Customers-not-yet-paid rises. Cash in December is **zero**. The profit is real in the way the statements mean real, and absent in the way Sabine means real, which is the way wages are real.

The argument in the room is rarely about whether a delivered truck can be revenue. A junior who says "you cannot book it until they pay" has confused cash with profit, and will mis-teach every later chapter, because every later chapter eats profit as an input. The argument is about the dent. Tools come back. Not often, and often enough that a silent year is a lie. The last three years of returns and repairs cost about 4 percent of sales. Four percent of €900,000 is **€36,000**. Gross profit after that provision is **€324,000**. Still no cash.

The provision is not a cheque. It is a debt to the future, a guess with a name. Guessing is allowed. Guessing zero, when the last three years were not zero, is a choice, and it is a choice with a beneficiary. The salesperson wants the sale in this year. Martin wants the year to look like the year he promised Klaus in January. Neither of them is a villain. Both of them are paid, in reputation or in a bonus or in a quiet way at Sunday lunch, on the photograph. A photograph with a known cost left out is a flattering photograph. The flattery is €36,000. Against €16,000,000 of gross profit it is **0.225 percent**. Tiny, and still enough to turn a missed promise into a kept one if the promise was missed by less than that. Size is not the test. The test is whether the page is what you say it is.

Jonas's "clean it up next year if the returns actually happen" moves a cost into a year where nobody is watching. It is an old trick because it works on people who look at one year at a time. It also dirties the next year before the next year has started. A provision you skip is a cost you have agreed to recognise later, in a year that did not cause it. The later year will want its own December truck. This is how a firm develops a permanent gap between the profit it announces and the cash it collects, and then acts surprised when the bank asks about the gap.

The honest sentence available in this scene, without a full cash-flow statement, is narrow and sufficient. The €324,000 is a claim on a customer and a reserve for repairs. On the morning of a January meeting the cash from this truck has not arrived. If the meeting is early in February, it still has not arrived. Profit is a statement about the year. Cash is a statement about the day. Anyone who wants a single number to do both jobs is about to be disappointed, or about to be misled, and the difference is whether they built the number.

Piotr's point about the commission is the point the accounting textbooks reach last and the factory reaches first. If the salesperson is paid when the truck leaves, the accounts will be asked to agree with the pay packet. People do not fake numbers for sport. They fake them, or they lean on them, because a packet depends on the lean. Pay the commission when the customer pays, and the argument about December becomes smaller, because the person with the incentive has been moved onto the same calendar as the cash. You will not always win that argument with a sales director. You should have it before you have the argument about the provision, because the provision is the symptom and the packet is the cause.

Four percent is a policy, not a measurement of this chain. A new customer might return nothing, or everything. The case should name the person who looks at returns in March and changes the 4 percent if it was a fairy tale. If that person does not exist, the provision is a paragraph you wrote to feel careful. Policies that nobody revisits are decoration. Decoration in a set of accounts is worse than a rough number someone owns, because the rough number can be improved and the decoration will be copied forward.

The tax office has its own view of when a sale is a sale, and chapter 10 is that bill. A profit you book in December can be taxed before the customer pays. The touch-up has a calendar. The audit, if there is one, will ask why the provision is 4 percent and whether last year's provision was used or simply rolled. You do not need to be the auditor. You need to be able to answer in a sentence that does not change between the meeting and the working paper.

## What she puts in the file

Book the truck. Book the dent. Revenue €900,000, cost €540,000, provision €36,000, gross profit €324,000, cash in December zero, cash expected in February if they pay. The flattery from skipping the provision is €36,000, which is 0.225 percent of the year's gross profit and is still large next to a promise missed by less.

The 4 percent is a policy from the last three years, not a fact about this chain. Someone is named to look at the actual returns in March. The salesperson's commission is proposed to follow the cash, not the truck. If that proposal loses, the provision matters more, not less, because the incentive to lean on December has been left in place.

Chapter 6's values and chapter 3's fence eat this profit. A kind estimate becomes a kind value and a calm covenant. Sabine will ask about cash. The bonus will not, unless someone rewrites the bonus.

## How the course marks a truck

The wrong answer, confidently given, is that a December delivery is "not a real sale." It is a real sale and unreal cash. The other wrong answer is that the provision is optional because it is an estimate. Estimates are where the year is made. Full marks require both lines, the zero cash, the 0.225 percent, and a sentence about who is paid when the number is booked. A student who computes €36,000 and then says it is too small to matter has failed the test the chapter set. The test was never the size. The test was the photograph.

## In the room

The question is the question behind every statement. What was estimated, and who benefits if the estimate is kind? The page the case leaves out is the pay packet tied to the photograph.
