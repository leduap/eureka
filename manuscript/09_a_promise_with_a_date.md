# 9. A forecast is a promise with a date

The calibration line was bought. The board minute, which Leonie drafted, said extra revenue of €1,500,000 a year. It is October. The line is running. The orders in hand and the honest guess for the first full year add up to €900,000.

Martin says, "Forecasts move. The decision was right on the day." He is half right, which is the dangerous half.

## The day it was right

On the day, cash coming back was €690,000 a year and the net present value at 6 percent was €430,708. That sentence can stay in the minute. It should not stay in the plan.

## The day it is not

Revenue €900,000. Cash costs do not shrink with it. The €720,000 is mostly two technicians and a service contract, and Martin hired the technicians. Depreciation is still €480,000. Operating profit on the line is

€900,000 − €720,000 − €480,000 = **−€300,000**.

Cash is not that bad, because depreciation is still not a cheque. Tax, if the losses can be used against the firm's other profit, saves 30 percent of €300,000, which is €90,000. Cash from the line is −€300,000 + €90,000 + €480,000 = **€270,000** a year. The ledger has the same figure: loss after tax −€210,000, plus depreciation €480,000.

**Write this down.** If you knew this on the morning of the decision, what was the net present value at 6 percent?

It is **−€1,338,484**. You would not have bought the line. "Right on the day" means the day was using €1,500,000. It does not mean the line is still a good buy. Those are different sentences, and Martin has joined them.

## What is left, which is the only question left

The €2,400,000 has been spent. Regret does not invoice. The question in October is whether the next five years of €270,000, and the €300,000 of parts at the end, are worth more than the line would fetch if you sold it.

A dealer will pay €1,500,000 for the installed line. The parts can be sold at what they cost. Stopping puts €1,500,000 + €300,000 = **€1,800,000** in the bank.

Keeping, at 6 percent, is the present value of five times €270,000 plus the parts in five years. That is **€1,361,500**, near enough. The ledger's unrounded figure is €1,361,516.

Stopping is ahead by €438,484. Not because the original decision looks foolish in the rear-view mirror. Because a person with a chequebook is offering more than the future cash.

**Your number.** Suppose the dealer offers €900,000 and the parts are unsaleable, worth something only if the line keeps running. Do you stop?

Keeping is still €1,361,516, and that figure included the parts coming back. If the parts are unsaleable outside the line, the dealer's €900,000 is the whole of stopping. You keep. The parts and the thin cash beat a low offer. The sunk cost still does not get a vote. The offer does.

## Three hands

Jonas says: never sell. "You will crystallise a loss the bank can see. Let it earn its way back."

Amina says: sell at €1,500,000, and write the minute as a new decision with new cash, not as an apology for the old one.

Piotr says: before either, ask why the revenue is €900,000. If the missing €600,000 is a late customer, the forecast is a date problem. If the missing €600,000 is a product nobody wants, the dealer is the best customer you are going to get.

Piotr first, then Amina. Jonas is protecting a feeling and calling it prudence. The bank can see the line either way, the day Sabine asks what it produces.

The Monday rule. **A forecast has a date on it. When the date arrives, make a new decision with the cash that is still yours to refuse.**

## Where the case goes quiet

The tax saving inside the €270,000 assumes the rest of Hartfeld has profit to set the line's loss against. If the whole firm is in the same hole, the tax saving waits, and the cash is worse than €270,000. Say so before you keep a weak line for its tax.

The other silence is the two technicians. Selling the line does not unhire them by itself. If their wages continue, they are no longer a cost of the line. They are a cost of the firm, and the dealer's offer looks even better. If their wages end with the line, they are already inside the €720,000 you took out by stopping. Count them once.

## Check

1. At €900,000 of revenue and €720,000 of cash cost, yearly cash from the line is €270,000, not negative.
2. Known at the start, the net present value at 6 percent is −€1,338,484. You would have refused it.
3. With the money spent, keeping is worth €1,361,516. Selling at €1,500,000 and recovering the parts at cost puts €1,800,000 in the bank. You stop.
4. The original cheque does not belong in that comparison.

## Exhibit 9. The decision you can still make

The original cheque is spent. It appears in neither column.

| | Keep the line | Stop, and take the dealer |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Cash each year, five years | 270,000 | — |
| Parts, at the end or now | 300,000 at the end | 300,000 now, at cost |
| Dealer, today | — | 1,500,000 |
| Worth today, at 6 percent | 1,361,516 | 1,800,000 |

Five payments of €270,000 at 6 percent are worth €1,137,338. The parts in five years are worth €224,177. Together, €1,361,516. The dealer plus the parts today is €1,800,000. Stopping is ahead by €438,484.

**Write this down.** The dealer cuts the offer to €900,000 and will not take the parts. The parts are worthless unless the line runs. Do you stop?

Stopping is now only €900,000. Keeping is still €1,361,516, and that figure needs the parts to come back through the line. You keep. The original €2,400,000 still does not get a vote. The offer got smaller, so the answer changed. That is what a new decision feels like. It is allowed to disagree with January.

## How the €270,000 was built

Revenue €900,000. Cash costs €720,000. Depreciation €480,000. Operating profit −€300,000. The loss saves €90,000 of tax against the rest of the firm's profit. Cash is −€300,000 + €90,000 + €480,000 = €270,000. If the rest of the firm has no profit to set the loss against, the €90,000 does not arrive this year, and keeping is worth less than the table says. Ask that question before you fall in love with a weak line for its tax.

## Two sentences Martin has joined

On the day the board minute was written, cash coming back was €690,000 a year and the net present value at 6 percent was €430,708. At the family's harsher 8 percent it was still €259,145. That sentence can stay in the minute. It describes a day, a forecast, and a decision. It should not stay in the plan as if October were still that day.

October's revenue, orders in hand plus the honest guess, is €900,000. The cash costs do not shrink with the disappointment. The €720,000 is mostly two technicians and a service contract, and the technicians have been hired. Depreciation is still €480,000, because the cheque has been written and the spreading continues whether the customers arrive or not. Operating profit on the line is €900,000 − €720,000 − €480,000 = **−€300,000**.

Cash is kinder than that loss, and the kindness is the same trick as chapter 1, run on a worse fact. Depreciation is still not a cheque. If the rest of the firm has profit, the loss saves tax: 30 percent of €300,000 is €90,000. Cash is −€300,000 + €90,000 + €480,000 = **€270,000**. The ledger's other route to the same figure is a loss after tax of €210,000, plus the €480,000 added back. Learn both routes. They are the same route. A person who thinks they disagree will "correct" a model that was already right.

If you had known €900,000 on the morning of the decision, the net present value at 6 percent would have been **−€1,338,484**. At 8 percent it is worse, **−€1,417,793**. You would not have bought the line. "Right on the day" means the day was using €1,500,000. It does not mean the line is still a good buy. Martin has joined those sentences because joining them protects the minute, and the minute has his name on it. Your job is to unjoin them without calling him a fool. The day can have been honest and the machine can now be the wrong machine to keep. Both can be true, which is the sentence families find hardest, because it refuses a villain.

## The only comparison that still has a pulse

The €2,400,000 has been spent. Regret does not invoice. It also does not belong in the comparison, and this is the point students nod at and then violate in the spreadsheet by putting the original cheque in both columns "for completeness." Completeness is how a sunk cost gets a vote. Leave it out of both columns. If it is in both, it changes nothing, and if someone later deletes it from only one, it changes the answer. Absence is safer than symmetry.

A dealer will pay €1,500,000 for the installed line. The parts can be sold at what they cost, €300,000. Stopping puts **€1,800,000** in the bank.

Keeping, at 6 percent, is five years of €270,000 plus the parts at the end. Five payments are worth **€1,137,338**. The parts in five years are worth **€224,177**. Together, **€1,361,516**. The chapter's "€1,361,500, near enough" is the same number said in a room. The file uses the ledger.

Stopping is ahead by **€438,484**. Not because January looks foolish in the mirror. Because a person with a chequebook is offering more than the future cash. That is a new decision. It is allowed to disagree with the minute. Amina's instruction is the right one: write it as a new decision with new cash, not as an apology. Apologies smuggle the sunk cost back in, because an apology is about the past and the decision is about the offer.

Now change the offer, because the room will. The dealer cuts it to €900,000 and will not take the parts. The parts are worthless unless the line runs. Stopping is only €900,000. Keeping is still €1,361,516, and that figure needs the parts to come back through the line. You keep. The original €2,400,000 still does not vote. The offer got smaller, so the answer changed. If that feels inconsistent, it is the opposite. A rule that gave the same answer at every offer would be a rule that was not looking at the offer. It would be looking at the family's pride.

## The questions that sit under the cash

Piotr asks why the revenue is €900,000 before he asks whether to sell. If the missing €600,000 is a late customer, the forecast is a date problem and the dealer is a panic. If the missing €600,000 is a product nobody wants, the dealer is the best customer you are going to get, and waiting for the forecast to "come back" is Jonas's sentence about not crystallising a loss. The bank can see the line either way, the day Sabine asks what it produces. A loss that has not been written down is not a loss that has been avoided. It is a loss that has been left in a machine.

The tax saving inside the €270,000 assumes the rest of Hartfeld has profit to set the line's loss against. If the whole firm is in the same hole, the €90,000 does not arrive this year. Keeping is then worth less than €1,361,516, and the dealer's offer looks better, not worse. Say so before you keep a weak line for its tax. A tax shield that needs the rest of the firm to be healthy is a shield you should test against a bad year, which is the same instinct as chapter 3, applied to a machine rather than to a loan.

The two technicians are the other double count. Selling the line does not unhire them by itself. If their wages continue, they are no longer a cost of the line. They are a cost of the firm, and they sit outside the comparison, which makes the dealer's offer look even better, because you were going to pay them anyway. If their wages end with the line, they are already inside the €720,000 you escape by stopping. Count them once. A room that counts them as a reason to keep the line ("we have already hired them") and also as a cost inside the €270,000 has used them twice, in the direction of keeping. Sunk wages are sunk. Future wages are a choice. The hire date is not a vote.

## What she puts in the file

The January minute stands as a record of January. At the forecast used that day, the line was a yes: €430,708 at 6 percent. That forecast is no longer the forecast.

At €900,000 of revenue the line produces €270,000 of cash a year, if the rest of the firm can use the tax loss. Known at the start, that would have been a no: −€1,338,484 at 6 percent.

The cheque is spent. Against a dealer at €1,500,000 plus parts at cost, stopping puts €1,800,000 in the bank and keeping is worth €1,361,516. Stop. Against a dealer at €900,000 who will not take the parts, keep. The answer is allowed to follow the offer.

Someone has to say whether the missing revenue is a late customer or a product. The file does not pretend to know. It refuses to wait for pride to decide.

## How the course marks a sunk cost

The classic failure is to add the €2,400,000 to the cost of keeping, or to refuse the dealer because selling "locks in" a loss. Locking in is a feeling about recognition. The cash offer is a fact about the future. Full marks require the rebuilt €270,000, the negative net present value as a record of what you would have done, and a comparison that contains the dealer and does not contain the original cheque. A student who keeps the line because January was a yes has learned the first chapter and missed this one. The first chapter's yes had a date on it. The date has arrived.

## In the room

The question is the forecast question, and the sunk-cost question hiding inside it. The page the case leaves out is a buyer for the remains, which is the only number that can still be negotiated.
