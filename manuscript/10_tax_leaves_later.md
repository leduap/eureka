# 10. Tax leaves on a different calendar

The profit before tax was €5,600,000. Leonie has been taxing it at 30 percent all year, which is €1,680,000, because 30 percent can be done in the margin of a meeting. Martin asks her, once, to stop being convenient.

## The Munich stack

A German company pays three layers, not one.

The corporate income tax rate is 15 percent. On top of that tax, a solidarity surcharge of 5.5 percent is still charged to companies, which is 0.825 percent of the profit. Munich's trade-tax multiplier is 490 percent. Applied to the 3.5 percent base, the trade tax is 17.15 percent. Add them, on a profit with no adjustments, and the stack is **32.975 percent**.

On €5,600,000 that is **€1,846,600**. The convenient 30 percent was €1,680,000. The difference is **€166,600**. Not enough to overturn the calibration line on the day it was a clear yes. Quite enough to overturn it on a day when the net present value was €50,000. Convenience has a direction. It makes projects look better than the city will.

**Write this down.** In chapter 1 the line's yearly operating profit was €300,000 and the cash was €690,000 at a 30 percent tax. What is the cash if the tax is 32.975 percent?

Tax becomes €300,000 × 0.32975 = €98,925. Profit after tax is €201,075. Add back depreciation of €480,000 and the cash is **€681,075**. You have lost about €9,000 a year. Over five years, discounted, the net present value shrinks and does not disappear. Do the shrinkage when the decision is close. Do not rebuild the whole model in the meeting when it is not.

The 30 percent stays the working rate of this book, so a reader can check the sums. The blue page says to swap in the city's rate before a close call, and to remember that trade tax is not the same in Stuttgart as in Munich. The multiplier is a municipal decision. It is not a law of nature.

## The months the cash actually leaves

Profit tax is computed on a year. The cash leaves during the year, in instalments set from what you earned before, and then a settling-up after the return.

Trade-tax instalments are due on 15 February, 15 May, 15 August and 15 November. Corporate tax instalments are due on 10 March, 10 June, 10 September and 10 December. Four slices of the convenient €1,680,000 would be €420,000 each, and the real stack is larger. The dates are the point, not the slice. A customer who pays in ninety days and a tax office that is paid in February are not on speaking terms.

The December truck from chapter 8 is the clean example. Gross profit after the provision was €324,000. Cash in December was zero. A slice of tax on that profit can be due while the invoice is still a receivable. The sale was not a mistake. The cash plan that ignored the tax date was.

Depreciation is the one kindness in the calendar. The €1,200,000 of depreciation on the existing plant does not leave the bank, and it lowers the tax. At 30 percent the shield is **€360,000** a year. That is why chapter 1 added depreciation back after charging tax on a profit that had already deducted it. You have seen this trick before. It is the same trick.

## Three hands

Jonas says: use 30 percent until the end of the model, then add a line. "Precision before the decision is theatre."

Amina says: use 33 percent for anything you might actually sign, and 30 percent for learning the shape. Two models, one of them honest.

Piotr says: he wants the four dates on the cash calendar next to the customer's ninety days. "I do not need another rate. I need to know whether February is a problem."

Piotr has the cash. Amina has the close call. Jonas is right only when the decision is not close, and he does not know that until someone has used the harsher rate once.

The Monday rule. **Tax is a cash outflow with a due date. A rate without a date is a classroom rate.**

## Where the case goes quiet

The tax base is not the profit in the annual report. Trade tax adds some things back and takes some things off. Interest, in particular, does not always shield tax the way chapter 3 hoped. The 30 cents per euro of debt was a teaching shield. The Munich shield is smaller, and some of the interest is added back. If a decision rests on the shield, it rests on a tax adviser. This chapter is how you know to call one.

Losses can be carried to other years, within rules that change. A loss that the model offsets against this year's profit may actually be a loss that waits. Waiting is a discount-rate problem. A shield next year is not a shield today.

## Check

1. The Munich stack, with no adjustments, is 15 + 0.825 + 17.15 = 32.975 percent.
2. On €5,600,000 the convenient tax is €1,680,000 and the stacked tax is €1,846,600.
3. The line's yearly cash falls from €690,000 to €681,075 at the stacked rate. A clear yes stays a yes.
4. Depreciation of €1,200,000 shields €360,000 a year at the 30 percent working rate.

**Write this down.** Depreciation on the existing plant is €1,200,000. How much tax does that save at the working rate, and does the saving require a cheque?

It saves €1,200,000 × 30 percent = **€360,000**, and it requires no cheque. That is why the cash of a project adds depreciation back after tax has been charged on a profit that already deducted it. You have used this once, in chapter 1. Use it the same way every time, or you will reject a machine for a cost you already paid.
