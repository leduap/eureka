# One hour, with the folder shut

Leonie takes the train home with the folder shut. This is the hour. Twelve questions, no looking back. Write the answer in a line. Then read the line under it.

If you get nine, you can sit in the meeting. If you get six, reread the blue pages and only the blue pages. If you get twelve, you are ready to be bored by the right things, which is the job.

**1.** The calibration line produces €300,000 of operating profit and €690,000 of cash. Why are those not the same number?

Revenue €1,500,000, cash costs €720,000, depreciation €480,000. The depreciation is the €2,400,000 cheque spread over five years. It was deducted to reach the profit, and it is not a second cheque. Tax at 30 percent on €300,000 is €90,000. Profit after tax is €210,000. Cash adds the depreciation back: €210,000 + €480,000 = €690,000. If you discounted the €300,000, you rejected a machine for a cost you had already counted. If your net present value was negative, that is the usual reason. The ledger's value at 8 percent, done on the €690,000, is €259,145. At 6 percent it is €430,708. At 10 percent it is still a yes, €101,919. The year-by-year euros at 8 percent, if you do not trust the factor 3.99271, are €638,889, €591,564, €547,744, €507,171 and €469,602. They add to the same place. Five undiscounted cash flows are €3,450,000, and the surplus if you also count the parts and ignore time is €1,050,000. That surplus is not a method. Payback, which turns positive only at the end of year four, by €60,000, is not a method either.

**2.** The line's net present value is positive and the bank balance goes to €300,000 overdrawn. Which fact stops the purchase?

Neither, by itself. Cash in the firm is €2,400,000. The machines take all of it. The parts take €300,000 the firm does not have, and the first customers have not paid. Interest on that hole for four months at 5 percent is €5,000, which does not overturn €259,145. The overdraft stops the purchase only if nobody will cover those months. Name Sabine, or name whoever has replaced her, before you call it a yes. A value is not a deposit. If the revenue were only €1,200,000, you would refuse for a different reason: the value is −€579,324 at 8 percent and −€453,888 at 6 percent. That is a bad project. The overdraft on the good project is a calendar.

**3.** Why is the smart gauge not allowed to use Hartfeld's 6 percent?

The 6 percent belongs to a beta of 0.90, a cost of equity of 7.5 percent, an after-tax debt cost of 3.5 percent, and book weights on €13,200,000 and €8,000,000. The gauge is a different risk. At 6 percent its net present value is €179,811. At 12 percent it is −€213,898. The cash did not change. The claim about the cash changed. A beta of 1.60 is a judgment you label, not a regression you pretend to have run. There is no share price. Even if Klaus pushes the firm's own beta to 1.20, the weighted average is 6.92 percent, and his old 8 percent hurdle is still harsher than the method. The false yes on the gauge is the expensive error. The false hurdle on the caliper makes the firm slow. Both are one rate pasted onto every idea in the building.

**4.** You valued the firm at the 6 percent weighted average. Do you add 30 percent of the debt on top?

No. Thirty percent of €8,000,000 is €2,400,000, and that shield is already inside the 6 percent, because 5 percent of debt became 3.5 percent after tax. Add it again and you have counted the bank twice. The other home for the shield is a valuation at the asset rate, about 6.16 percent, which puts the unlevered firm near €68,200,000 and the levered firm near €70,600,000. That machine adds the shield once, at the end, and you say "permanent" when you use it. You do not run both machines.

**5.** Extra debt of €6,000,000 looks fine this year. What is the net-debt ratio if EBITDA falls to €4,000,000 and the cash does not recover?

Debt becomes €14,000,000. Interest is €730,000: the old €8,000,000 stays at 5 percent, and only the new money costs 5.5 percent. Cash €2,400,000 still leaves net debt of €11,600,000. Divided by €4,000,000 that is 2.90. The fence was 2.0. Operating profit in that year is €2,800,000, and cover is 3.84, under the floor of 4. This year's 1.61 and 8.2 are the weather you already had, plus a loan. Room for new debt, working backwards from a cover of 4, is €5,454,545, and only if cash holds. If cash falls another €1,000,000, net debt is €12,600,000 and the ratio is worse. The offer is a good-year offer.

**6.** Ingrid is paid a dividend of €240,000 gross. Has the family become richer?

No. The firm paid out €1,200,000. She received her fifth. The money changed rooms. She is richer, the firm is poorer by the same cash, and if the lecture's 26.375 percent applies she keeps €176,700 after tax of €63,300, so the family as a whole holds less than the firm gave up. That tax is a pencil figure. A 20 percent holder is often not in the simple box. If she is the only seller at €42.40, the cash in her hand can match and her stake falls to about 17.7 percent, which is a different decision, an exit in part, not a dividend with a clever name. And the machines wanted €2,700,000. The cheque and the line do not fit in the same month.

**7.** Nordmess falls 9 percent on a headline you read an hour later. What do you buy?

Nothing, on the strength of the headline. The price has already held the meeting. The drop is the market subtracting a contract, not a sale laid on for you. A fund that tracks an index buys a new member because it promised to, at that day's price. That is a specification, not an edge. A phone call to a workshop that has lost a supplier is commercial work. It does not require you to own the shares, and it does not become smarter because the shares fell.

**8.** Steady cash says the equity is worth €64,400,000. The peer's multiple says €42,400,000. What do you tell Klaus to say out loud?

Both, and why they differ. Not the average, and not €59,000,000. Steady cash of €4,200,000 at 6 percent is a business of €70,000,000, then net debt of €5,600,000 comes off. Eight times operating profit of €6,000,000 is a business of €48,000,000, and the same debt comes off. €4,200,000 divided by €48,000,000 is 8.75 percent. The multiple is an 8.75 percent buyer, or a buyer who does not believe the cash lasts. Ingrid's 20 percent is €12,880,000 or €8,480,000. At 25 percent off the multiple, because her buyer cannot sell on Monday, it is €6,360,000, and that discount is a negotiation. Last year is a photograph. You have not promised it will continue unless you say so.

**9.** A €6,000,000 order has €2,400,000 of gross profit. How much cash does it tie up for ninety days?

€3,600,000, the cost, not the profit. The profit is still in the customer's pocket. Interest at 5 percent is €44,384. The order survives that cost: €2,355,616 remains before overhead and tax. The firm's cash of €2,400,000 does not cover the €3,600,000, so Sabine is in the sentence. Giving up €80,000 of price to save thirty days saves €14,795 of interest and does not pay on interest alone. Ordinary cash is already out for 73 days. The scrap, if a special order is cancelled, is the larger question, and it is not in the interest formula.

**10.** A 28 December delivery raises gross profit and raises cash in December. True or false?

False. It can raise the profit. Revenue €900,000 and cost €540,000 is gross profit of €360,000, and cash in December is zero. A 4 percent provision is €36,000, so the honest gross profit is €324,000, and the cash still arrives when the customer pays. Skipping the provision flatters the year by €36,000, which is 0.225 percent of €16,000,000 and is large next to a promise missed by less than that. A covenant, a multiple and a bonus built on the flattered profit inherit it. The person paid when the truck leaves is the reason the photograph leans.

**11.** The line is disappointing. The €2,400,000 is spent. A dealer offers more for the remains than the remaining cash is worth. What does the original cheque get to do?

Nothing. It is spent in either future. At €900,000 of revenue the line's cash is €270,000 a year, and known at the start the net present value at 6 percent is −€1,338,484. That sentence belongs in the file as a record of what you would have done. It does not decide October. Keeping is worth €1,361,516. The dealer plus the parts at cost is €1,800,000. Stopping is ahead by €438,484. If the dealer offers only €900,000 and the parts are unsaleable off the line, you keep, and the original cheque still does not vote. The offer got smaller, so the answer was allowed to change.

**12.** The customer wants, for free, the right to double the order only when doubling hurts you. What do you write on the quote?

A price. The weight that matches the safe rate is 0.45, not a forecast and not a coin. The right is worth €175,610, which is 7.3 percent of the €2,400,000 gross profit. Halve the pain and it is still €87,805. "They will probably not use it" is not "it is worth nothing." Charge, cap, or refuse. A monthly option sold for income, against shares or against cash set aside to buy shares, is not this decision. The chain is a customer. The premium you do not charge comes out of the tools.

Shut the folder again. The blue pages are the ones that go to work in the morning. The pack is how the twelve of them share a table. The rest is how you came to believe them.
